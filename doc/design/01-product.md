# The product

## What Ascent is

Ascent is a civic simulation of approval voting. In approval voting a voter approves as many candidates as they like, and the candidate approved by the most voters wins. Ascent adds a step before the approval: each voter rates every candidate in a race on a scale from 0 to 500, then sets a **baseline**. Every candidate rated above the baseline is approved. Drawn as a chart, the ratings rise and fall across the field like a range of peaks, so each voter's view of a race is called their **mountain**.

The goal is to let communities, researchers and voters try this before any real election changes its rules. Ascent holds real places, offices and 2026 contests alongside mock and historical elections, so approval voting can be tried on the races voters actually face, not only on invented ones.

## The name

**Ascent** is the climb up the mountain, and the rise of a politician's career. It is also a homophone of **assent**: to agree to or approve of something after thoughtful consideration, which is exactly what an approval is. The domain, **ascent.ing**, reads as "ascenting", which sounds like both "assenting" and "ascending". The name, the metaphor and the voting method are one idea.

## The ideas every screen has to make clear

- **Rating is not voting.** A rating says how much a voter likes a candidate. The vote comes from the baseline, so moving the baseline changes the approval without changing any rating.
- **Approving more than one candidate is normal.** Similar candidates can share support instead of splitting it. Ascent wants that shared popularity to be visible, especially for candidates who did not win.
- **Primaries are part of the field.** A primary is a race for a party's nomination, or for a place in a top-two or top-four primary. Everyone who ran in a primary, including those who lost, can appear in the general election's field as an off-ballot candidate. Each voter chooses, race by race, whether to include them; they are hidden by default.
- **Policy belongs to offices.** Topics, issues, approaches and stances connect to real offices and jurisdictions, so a voter can ask whether the office a candidate seeks can actually do what the candidate promises.
- **Real, mock and historical elections sit side by side,** and the interface must always say which one a voter is looking at.

## Who it is for

From the product requirements:

| Person | What they need | What they use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civic educator | Teaching tools, historical examples, student engagement | Mock elections, simulations |
| Organization administrator | Secure, auditable voting and member management | Binding elections, results |
| Political enthusiast | Nuanced preferences and deep candidate analysis | Mountains, multi-dimensional evaluation |
| Strategic voter | The most impact from their vote | Polls, baseline strategy, behavior models |
| Overwhelmed voter | Simple information and trusted recommendations | Personalized education, combined mountains |
| Party official | Endorsements and their candidates | Multi-endorsement tools, analytics |

The overwhelmed voter is the hardest case and the most important one: a 2026 general can have a dozen candidates on the ballot and more from the primaries.

## What exists today

- **Places:** countries, states, counties (and Connecticut's planning regions), municipalities and districts, as one hierarchy of jurisdictions.
- **Contests:** years, elections, races, offices and positions, with real 2026 federal, statewide and state-legislative races; primaries as races for nomination offices.
- **People:** people, candidacies, ballot lines, parties and other organizations (unions, newspapers and so on), and endorsements.
- **Policy:** topics, issues, approaches, stances and the evidence behind them, plus governing bodies, policies and official codes.
- **Voting:** the mountain, where a signed-in voter rates candidates, sets a baseline and saves; approval results for completed races.
- **Engagement:** on almost every record a visitor can save, favorite, follow, hide or share it, and a Saved page collects what they marked.
- **Accounts:** sign-in with email or Google. Mountains are private to voters and admins by default. A voter can make individual saved mountains public, sharing their name, ratings, and baseline on a read-only page with a PNG preview. Unsaved drafts stay private. Admins also see controls to create, edit and delete reference data, and to simulate mountain data.

## What is planned

These are filed in Linear (ASC-54 to ASC-67) and will need screens: comments on records; daily limits on baseline changes, with warnings; sharing a mountain through verified links and QR codes; rating candidates on several dimensions (character, aptitude, process, endorsements, content); voter education built around a voter's own mountain; combining mountains from thought leaders and groups; endorsement tools for parties and organizations; and models of strategic voting. A redesign should leave room for them without designing them now.

## Tone

Civic and practical, never partisan. The home page puts it as voter agency, institutional fit, transparent experimentation and local usefulness. Ascent explains a voting method; it does not campaign for candidates or parties, and nothing in its color or language should read as favoring one.
